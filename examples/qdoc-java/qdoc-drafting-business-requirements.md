# QDoc drafting: business requirements

Prepared from the domain discovery session between the domain expert (Łukasz
Szydło) and the analyst (Wojciech Zaborowski). The session covered one part of
the QDoc system only: the drafting of a quality document, from the moment
somebody decides a document is needed until an approved version is ready to be
published. Publication, employee sign-off and archiving policy were explicitly
left for later sessions and appear here only where they shape the drafting
rules.

## 1. Why the system exists

Hospitals, factories, public offices and other organisations that hold or seek
a quality certificate (ISO or similar) are obliged to run a quality management
process. A large part of that process is producing written documents:
procedures, guidelines, instructions, policies. The system calls all of them
quality documents, or QDocs.

Today most organisations handle this by hand with spreadsheets, office
documents and e-mail. That is inconvenient and hard to audit. The product is
meant to be a ready-made system an organisation takes and uses, which
structures the process, automates the routine parts and keeps a complete
record of who did what and when.

The audit trail is not a nice-to-have. In a hospital, when a patient disputes
how something was done, the hospital defends itself by showing that a
procedure existed, that it was prepared and approved in a controlled way, and
that the doctor in question had seen and signed that exact version. The
system must make it impossible to credibly claim "nobody ever showed me that
document" or "that is not what I approved".

The system is not aimed at one industry. Any organisation that needs a
controlled document lifecycle is a target customer. Future ideas mentioned in
passing: ready-made document templates to speed up writing, and AI agents
acting as reviewers.

## 2. Domain vocabulary

**QDoc (quality document).** A procedure, guideline, instruction, policy or
similar document that the organisation must maintain. A QDoc is not a single
file. It is a package that always functions as a whole, like a bank loan
agreement with its annexes: signing the agreement means signing everything in
the package. A QDoc has a title, a type, a document number and one or more
versions. It is either active or archived.

**Document type.** Procedure, guideline, instruction, policy. The list may
grow.

**Document number (signature).** A human-facing identifier generated by the
system when the QDoc is created. Used for searching and for referring to the
document in conversation. It is not a security device and has nothing to do
with proving the content was not tampered with; that comes later, at
publication.

**Version.** A complete, self-contained edition of a QDoc. A version is what
goes through review, approval and publication. Versions carry the workflow
status; the QDoc itself does not. A version contains files.

**Content file.** A file the authors write in a rich-text editor. This is the
body of the document. A version must always have at least one content file.
Comments and edit history attach to content files.

**Attachment.** A supporting file added to a version. Attachments are PDF only,
are not edited inside the system and are downloaded rather than displayed.
Attachments are optional. Before it counts as part of the version an
attachment must pass a verification step (see section 6).

**Revision / change history.** The record of edits made to a content file's
content over time, within one version. The expert was explicit that this is
called history, not versioning, to avoid confusion with document versions.

**Activity list.** The per-document log of everything that happened to it:
who created it, who was assigned, who edited which file, who commented, who
approved, when. Comparable to the activity tab of an issue tracker.

**Quality manager.** The person responsible for the whole document lifecycle
in the organisation. Creates QDocs, assigns people to them, creates new
versions. Does not write content.

**Author (also called contributor).** The only role that may edit content
files and add attachments. Responsible for preparing the document. The expert
used "author" and "contributor" interchangeably and asked to standardise on
"author".

**Reviewer.** Reads the shared version and leaves comments. Optional; a
document may have none. Reviewers do not block the process. In the future a
reviewer may be an AI agent rather than a person.

**Approver.** Takes responsibility for the document by approving each of its
files. At least one is required before a version can be shared. Approvers may
close comments and, in the end, must approve; they cannot reject a document
outright.

**Auditor.** An external, accredited person who periodically inspects the
quality process. May create test documents in the system to watch the process
run.

**Employee (regular user).** The audience of a published document. Sees only
the current published version, never drafts, history or previous versions.

## 3. Roles and what they may do

| Role | Creates QDocs | Assigns people | Edits content | Adds attachments | Comments | Marks as reviewed | Approves files | Closes comments | Sees history |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quality manager | yes | yes | no | no | yes | no | no | no | yes |
| Auditor | yes (test documents) | yes, on own documents | no | no | yes | no | no | no | yes |
| Author | no | no | yes | yes | yes | no | no | no | yes |
| Reviewer | no | no | no | no | yes | yes | no | no | yes |
| Approver | no | no | no | no | yes | no | yes | yes | yes |
| Employee | no | no | no | no | no | no | no | no | no |

Some nuances behind the table:

- Only the quality manager may add authors. An author who wants a colleague
  on the document asks the quality manager outside the system; there is no
  in-system request. The expert initially allowed authors to add contributors,
  then reversed that during the session.
- The auditor is treated like a quality manager for their own documents. The
  difference is only in how the document number is formed and in the
  convention that such documents are published to a very narrow audience.
- Anyone assigned to a document may be a real person or, in the future, an
  automated actor (an AI reviewer, the system itself when it removes a file
  that failed scanning). The activity list must make clear which actions were
  taken by the system rather than by a person.

## 4. The lifecycle of a document

### 4.1 Creating a QDoc

The quality manager learns, by whatever means, that a new document is needed
and creates it in the system. The session considered and rejected the
alternative where the quality manager merely tells an author to go and create
it; the skeleton is always created by the quality manager (or auditor).

To create a QDoc the creator supplies:

- a title (required),
- a document type (required),
- at least one author (required),
- reviewers (optional, can be added later),
- approvers (optional at creation, but see 4.3).

When the QDoc is created the system automatically:

- generates the document number,
- creates version 1 with status new,
- creates the first, empty content file inside version 1 for the authors to
  fill in,
- records the creation in the activity list,
- notifies every quality manager in the organisation that a new document
  exists. Creating a procedure is a significant event and the other quality
  managers should know about it.

Until the version is shared for review, it is visible only to the quality
manager who created it and to the assigned authors.

### 4.2 Document numbers

Every QDoc receives a number of the form `<sequence>/<month>/<year>`, for
example `1/12/2025` for the first document created in December 2025. The
sequence part increases by one with each new document. The month and year are
the month and year of creation. The expert compared it to invoice numbering.

Customers frequently have their own conventions. They may want a prefix or
suffix so that document numbers do not collide with, say, their invoice
numbers. The number template must therefore be configurable per organisation.

On top of the core template, two situational rules apply:

- Documents created in a demo instance of the product carry a `demo` prefix.
  The purpose is to make it visible that a demo is being used, so that nobody
  quietly runs real quality documents through a trial installation.
- Documents created by a user in the auditor role carry an auditor suffix.
  This marks the document as non-binding: it exists so the auditor can watch
  the process, not to become a real procedure.

### 4.3 Assigning people

The quality manager may add authors, reviewers and approvers at any time
while the document is not archived. Every assignment records who was assigned,
by whom and when in the activity list, and the assigned person should be
notified.

Rules that apply to every assignment, whichever role:

- The person doing the assigning must be the quality manager (or the auditor
  for their own document).
- The assigned person must exist in the system, must hold the role they are
  being assigned to, and must be an active user. A blocked or deactivated
  account cannot be assigned.
- The document must not be archived.
- If all conditions hold, the person appears on the document's list for that
  role; if not, the assignment is refused. The session noted the refusal does
  not need a polished user experience, since it mostly arises from misuse or
  from a role being withdrawn between the assignment and its acceptance.

The session discussed an invitation model, as used by GitHub, where the
assigned person receives an invitation and the checks run when they accept it.
This was left as one of several ways to meet the rules above, all of which
must give the same business outcome.

### 4.4 Working on the content

Once a version exists, the authors work on its content files. The work is
collaborative: several authors may edit at the same time, in a rich-text
editor. The system keeps the change history of every content file from the
moment the file was created in that version to the present, so that anyone on
the drafting team can see who changed what and when.

Authors may add further content files and attachments, and may remove files,
as long as the version keeps at least one content file. A version with no
attachments is fine; a version with no content file is not allowed.

There is no separate "in progress" or "in review" status on the version.
Sharing the version with reviewers and approvers is not a hand-over after
which the authors stop working. The expert described it as editing a book:
reviewers and approvers join the work and comment, but only the authors change
the text. A version is therefore new from creation until it is approved,
possibly with a flag recording that it has been shared.

### 4.5 Sharing for review

The authors decide when the draft is far enough along to share. Sharing means
the assigned reviewers and approvers can now see the version and comment on
it. From the authors' side, sharing is their statement that, in their view,
the document is ready to be approved.

Before a version can be shared, at least one approver must be assigned.
Reviewers remain optional.

After sharing:

- Reviewers and approvers comment on fragments of content files.
- Authors respond by editing, which they may continue to do freely.
- A reviewer may mark the version as reviewed, meaning "I have read it and
  have no objections". This is informational. It exists so that an approver
  can see whether the reviewers have had their say before approving, and can
  chase a silent reviewer outside the system if they want to.
- A reviewer who says nothing is treated as silently accepting. Approval does
  not wait for reviewers.

### 4.6 Approval

Approval is given file by file, not on the version as a whole. Every assigned
approver must approve every file. The expert acknowledged that in most cases a
version will have a single content file, so this is not burdensome, and that
per-section approval may become necessary in industries with very long
documents (aviation manuals were the example), but that is out of scope.

To approve a file:

- the approver must be assigned to the document,
- the file must be active, meaning an attachment must have completed its
  verification (section 6),
- there must be no open comments on the file. An approver must explicitly
  close every comment on a file before approving it. Closing a comment is the
  approver's acknowledgement that it was seen, whether or not it led to a
  change. The expert first allowed approval over open comments, then decided
  the explicit closing was the better product decision.
- the approver must be approving exactly the content they were looking at.
  If the content changed under them since they opened it, the approval must
  not go through.

Once a file has been approved its content is locked; authors can no longer
edit it. Consequently there is no scenario where an edit invalidates an
earlier approval.

When the last approver approves the last file, the version automatically
becomes approved and is ready for publication, which is handled by a separate
part of the system.

An approver cannot reject a document. The reasoning: if the quality manager
has decided the organisation needs the procedure (a new X-ray machine has
arrived and the radiologists cannot work without an operating procedure), then
the procedure must come into existence. The approver's job is to make sure the
content is fit for purpose, by commenting and pushing back until it is, and
then to take responsibility for it by approving. Disagreement is expressed
through comments and conversation, not through a reject action.

Future ideas noted but not required now: configurable approval strategies
(everyone, at least one per file, majority), chosen by the quality manager
when creating the document; an auto-approve policy in place of a list of
approvers. The analyst pointed out that a majority strategy would need some
form of explicit objection so nobody is outvoted silently; parked until the
need is real.

### 4.7 New versions

A published version cannot be changed. Until publication, even after approval,
a late correction is still possible (rare, but it happens between approval and
distribution). Once employees have been given a version, any change, however
small, requires a new version, and the new version goes through the entire
review and approval process again. Each version is a complete document in its
own right; a version that merely "adds one sentence" still contains the whole
text.

The example used: a software testing procedure, version 1, describes manual
testing. A new CTO switches the company to automated testing. It is still the
same procedure with the same number; it gets version 2.

Rules for new versions:

- A new version can be created only when the previous version is published
  and the QDoc is not archived. There can never be two versions of the same
  document in preparation at once, nor two published at once.
- The new version starts with status new and is a copy of all files of the
  previous version, content files and attachments alike, so the authors edit
  rather than start from nothing. The number of files may then diverge from
  the previous version.
- Authors, reviewers and approvers are assigned afresh for the new version.
  Because years may pass between versions, the people on the previous version
  may have left or changed roles. The user interface may pre-fill the form with
  the previous version's people as a convenience, but each of them is subject
  to the normal assignment checks.
- Creating a new version notifies all quality managers, as creating a
  document does.

### 4.8 Archiving

A QDoc can be archived so that it stops cluttering people's lists. This is a
convenience for users, not a step in the process. A QDoc may be archived only
when none of its versions is still in force or in preparation. An archived
QDoc accepts no further assignments or versions. Deletion was mentioned as
distinct from archiving and was not discussed further.

## 5. Visibility and the audit trail

Who sees what:

- Before sharing: the creating quality manager and the assigned authors.
- After sharing: additionally the assigned reviewers and approvers.
- Change history of content files and previous versions: quality managers,
  authors, reviewers and approvers of the document. Never regular employees.
- Employees see only the current published version, and only from the point
  where the publication part of the system hands it to them.

The activity list of a document must record at least: creation, every
assignment (who, by whom, when), every content change, every comment, every
"mark as reviewed", every approval, every file added or removed, every version
created, and every action taken automatically by the system (for instance an
attachment removed after a failed scan). Status transitions must not get lost;
somebody reading the list later should never be surprised that something
happened without a trace.

## 6. Attachments

Attachments are PDF files only. They are added by authors and are not edited
in the system; reviewers and approvers download them, read them and either
comment elsewhere or approve them.

Uploaded attachments are a potential attack vector and a potential channel for
inappropriate content. Every uploaded attachment therefore goes into
quarantine until it has been checked for malware and for unacceptable content
(the example given was offensive images), and possibly for size. Only once the
checks pass does the attachment become active. Until then:

- nobody can download it,
- it cannot be approved,
- and so the version cannot become approved.

The organisation expects scanning to complete within a few minutes of upload.
Because authors normally share a version days after adding attachments, the
window in which a reviewer could hit an unscanned file is considered a rare
edge case, but the rule stands.

What happens when a scan fails was left as an open product decision between
two options:

1. Show a status on each attachment (checking, ready, failed) in the document
   view, and let the author decide what to do with a failed file.
2. Remove the failed attachment automatically, record the removal in the
   activity list as an action by the system, and e-mail the author explaining
   what was found and that the file was removed.

The analyst leaned towards visible statuses on the grounds that the approver
should see at a glance that every attachment is ready, and that the author
should make a conscious decision. The expert leaned towards automatic removal
plus notification to avoid keeping unusable files on screen. Either way the
audit trail must show the outcome.

Size limits for a single attachment and for all attachments of a version were
raised and not decided; a short piece of research on sensible limits and on
available scanning tools was agreed.

A related idea: recording that a reviewer or approver actually downloaded an
attachment, in the same way that downloading an insurance policy online counts
as having read it. This would let the system show that the people approving a
version have opened its attachments. Not committed to.

## 7. Notifications

Required in this part of the process:

- All quality managers in the organisation when a QDoc is created.
- All quality managers when a new version of a QDoc is created.
- A person when they are assigned to a document (implied by the invitation
  discussion; the exact form was not settled).
- The author when an attachment fails scanning, if the automatic-removal
  option is chosen.

## 8. Scenarios

**Standard document.** The quality manager of a software company creates
"Web application testing procedure", type procedure, with one author. The
system numbers it `1/12/2025`, creates version 1 with one empty content file,
and tells the other quality managers. The author writes the procedure over a
few days, uploads one PDF checklist, and shares the version once the quality
manager has added two reviewers and one approver. One reviewer comments that a
section is missing; the author adds it. The other reviewer marks it as
reviewed without comments. The approver closes the remaining comment, approves
the content file and the attachment, and the version becomes approved.

**Later change.** Two years on, the company drops manual testing. The quality
manager creates version 2 of `1/12/2025`. It starts as a copy of version 1's
files. The form pre-fills the old author, reviewers and approver; one reviewer
has left the company and cannot be assigned, so the quality manager picks a
replacement. Version 2 goes through review and approval; version 1 remains the
one in force until version 2 is published.

**Late correction.** A version is approved but not yet published. Someone
spots a typo. Because nobody outside the drafting team has received it, the
correction is made in place rather than through a new version. (The session
stated this as allowed; how it interacts with the lock that approval places on
a file was not resolved and is listed under open questions.)

**Second author.** An author realises the document needs a colleague's input.
They ask the quality manager, who adds the colleague as an author. The
colleague must already hold the author role and have an active account;
otherwise the assignment is refused. The addition appears in the activity
list with the date and the quality manager's name.

**Audit.** An external auditor logs in to check that the process works. They
create a test procedure; its number carries the auditor suffix. They assign a
member of the audited team as author, approver and eventually the sole
recipient, and watch the document travel through drafting and approval. The
document is real in every respect except its number and, by convention, its
audience. The system does not hide or filter it.

**Demo.** A prospective customer is given a demo installation. Every document
they create is numbered with a `demo` prefix, so a document from the demo can
never be mistaken for a binding one.

**Suspicious attachment.** An author uploads a PDF that fails the malware
check. The file never becomes downloadable or approvable. Depending on the
option chosen, either the author sees it flagged as failed and removes it, or
the system removes it, logs that it did so, and e-mails the author.

**Approval attempt with open comments.** An approver tries to approve a
content file that still has a reviewer's open comment. The approval is
refused. The approver reads the comment, closes it, and approves.

## 9. Open questions and decisions still to make

- Attachment scan failure: statuses in the document view versus automatic
  removal with notification (section 6).
- Attachment size limits, per file and per version.
- Whether the drafting view should show each attachment's verification status
  even if the source of that status lives elsewhere.
- Whether to track that reviewers and approvers have downloaded attachments.
- How assignment is confirmed: immediate assignment with checks, or an
  invitation the assigned person accepts. The business rules are the same in
  both cases.
- How a late correction between approval and publication is performed, given
  that approval locks the file.
- Whether "shared for review" is a visible state of a version or only a fact
  recorded in the activity list.
- Which document types are in the initial list.
- Configuration of the document-number template per organisation: what parts
  may be customised.

## 10. Explicitly out of scope for this part

- Publication of an approved version, selection of the employees who must
  read it, conversion to a signed PDF and integration with e-signature
  services.
- Employee acknowledgement and signing.
- Document templates.
- AI reviewers.
- Approval strategies other than "every approver approves every file".
- Per-section review and approval.
- Deletion of documents.
